North Star Experience Canvas

Turn an opportunity into a future experience that people can understand at a glance.

Worked example · fictional company

NorthStar GmbH · Three product teams (procurement, the supplier portal and analytics) ship every month. You lead design across them, and want one standard for what ships and a protected space for what comes next.

How to use it

Fill it in at the start of an exploration. Write short, plain answers. If it needs a long explanation, it is not ready.

1. The canvas

Opportunity

What are we trying to improve or reinvent?

Cut the time buyers spend on routine requests for quotes.

Target user

Who benefits from this future experience?

Buyers in procurement who raise more than ten requests a week.

Current experience

What happens today?

Buyers fill a form for each request, send it, and chase replies by email.

Future experience

What should happen instead?

The assistant notices a need, drafts the request, and asks the buyer to approve it in one step.

North Star principle

What fundamental belief drives it?

People decide. Software prepares.

Key capabilities

What technology or capabilities enable it?

Reading of purchase history, a drafting agent, and a clear explanation of each suggestion.

Experience journey

How will it unfold?

Buyer sees a suggestion. Reads the reason. Approves, edits or declines. Replies arrive in one place.

Expected impact

What changes for customers or the business?

Hours saved each week, fewer missed orders, and more time for supplier relationships.

Key assumptions

What must be true for this to work?

Buyers will trust suggestions if they can see the reason. History data is good enough to predict needs.

Experimentation Canvas

Turn a North Star idea into the smallest experiment that will teach you something.

Worked example · fictional company

NorthStar GmbH · Three product teams (procurement, the supplier portal and analytics) ship every month. You lead design across them, and want one standard for what ships and a protected space for what comes next.

How to use it

Fill it in before every experiment, and again when it ends. A result that disproves your hypothesis is a good result.

1. The experiment

Hypothesis

What do we believe?

Buyers will approve a drafted request faster than they write one, if they can see the reason.

Assumption

What are we unsure about?

Whether buyers trust a suggestion enough to approve it without checking everything.

Experiment

The smallest thing we can build or test

Show eight buyers a clickable prototype with three suggestions, and watch what they do.

Prototype

What will we create?

A prototype of the suggestion and approval screens, with real purchase history from a pilot plant.

Target users

Who will test it?

Eight buyers from two plants, at least four who raise more than ten requests a week.

Success criteria

What evidence would show success?

Six of eight approve at least two suggestions without opening the history, and say they would use it.

Duration

How long will it run?

Two weeks.

Resources

Who and what do we need?

One designer, one prototyper, one researcher, and access to anonymised purchase history.

Learnings

What did we discover? Fill in at the end.

Seven of eight approved quickly when a reason was shown. Two wanted to see the price history first.

Decision

Stop, iterate or scale, and why

Iterate. Add the price history to the reason, then test again with a live pilot.

Experiment-to-Scale Assessment

Decide whether an experiment should stop, continue, be incubated or scale into a product.

Worked example · fictional company

NorthStar GmbH · Three product teams (procurement, the supplier portal and analytics) ship every month. You lead design across them, and want one standard for what ships and a protected space for what comes next.

How to use it

Use it when an experiment ends. Score each area from 1 to 5, record the evidence and the risks, and end with a clear decision.

1. The experiment

The experiment and when it ended

Proactive RFQ suggestions, ended on 24 October.

2. Score

AssessmentScore (1 to 5)Why
Customer desirability4Seven of eight buyers approved quickly and asked when they could use it.
Business relevance4Targets the time-to-send goal for this year.
Technical feasibility3Needs reliable access to purchase history, which two plants do not yet have.
Experience differentiation4Nobody else in our market does this yet.
Strategic alignment4Fits the plan for agent-assisted procurement.
Scalability and reusability3The explanation pattern can be reused. The suggestion logic is specific to RFQs.

3. Evidence and next steps

What evidence supports the concept?

Eight buyer sessions, a clear pattern in the approval behaviour, and an engineer’s view that it is feasible.

What risks remain?

Price history may be needed to earn trust. Two plants lack clean data.

What dependencies exist?

Access to purchase history, and a place in the procurement roadmap.

Which product team could adopt it?

The procurement team, where the agent pilot already runs.

What further investment is needed?

About two engineers for a quarter, and data clean-up in two plants.

The recommended next step

Incubate with the procurement team in the next quarter, and reuse the explanation pattern in the design system.

4. The decision

Do not reward a high rate of experiments reaching adoption. Learning that an idea does not work is a valuable result.
